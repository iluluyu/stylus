# Paper Structure (Whitesides) — codes P1–P7

**Core idea:** a paper presents ideas, not data. Data are the evidence for the ideas. Organize the paper as an outline of claims before writing prose, and fix structure before polishing sentences.

---

## P1 — Main message statable in one sentence

Before writing or revising, state the paper's main message in one or two sentences. If this is impossible, the problem is structural, not linguistic. Flag it before editing any sentences.

- Self-check: *If a colleague reads only one sentence, which sentence should it be?*

## P2 — Outline in claims, not topic labels

Each outline line is a full sentence that asserts something.

- Label: *Error mitigation*
- Claim: *Zero-noise extrapolation reduces the energy bias of the 12-qubit VQE run by 60%.*

Start the outline early, ideally while the research is still running. Gaps in the outline reveal missing experiments, simulations, or controls.

- Self-check: *Does every outline line contain a verb and a claim someone could disagree with?*

## P3 — Each figure supports one claim

Decide the figures, tables, and key equations first. Write one sentence per figure stating what it shows.

- A figure without a clear claim → move it to the Supplemental Material, or delete it.
- A claim without a figure or other evidence → `[EVIDENCE?]`.
- Self-check: *Can I state each figure's message in one sentence?*

## P4 — Introduction arc

1. **Context:** why the problem matters, in terms any physicist can follow.
2. **Known:** what has been done, with references.
3. **Gap:** what remains unsolved, stated specifically.
4. **Contribution:** "Here we ..." plus the key result.

Common faults:
- Opening too broadly (*With the rapid development of quantum computing, ...*).
- A literature list with no clear gap.
- The contribution buried at the end of a long paragraph.

- Self-check: *Can the reader find the gap and the "Here we" sentence in under ten seconds?*

## P5 — One paragraph, one point

The opening sentence states the issue; the closing sentence delivers the point. Do not end a paragraph on a side detail. For letters with tight length limits (e.g., PRL), outline at the paragraph level.

- Self-check: *If I keep only the first and last sentences, does the paragraph still make its point?*

## P6 — Conclusions add meaning, not repetition

State what was shown, what it means, the main limitations, and the outlook. Do not repeat the abstract, and do not introduce new results.

- Self-check: *Does the conclusion tell the reader something the abstract did not?*

## P7 — Abstract structure

Template (adapted from Nature's summary-paragraph guidance; compress for PRL):

1. Broad context any physicist can follow (1 sentence)
2. More specific background (1–2)
3. The problem or gap (1)
4. Main result, starting "Here we show / demonstrate / report ..." (1)
5. What is new compared with prior work, with key numbers (1–2)
6. Broader implication (1)

Avoid citations and undefined abbreviations in the abstract. Check the target journal's current length limits.

- Self-check: *Does the abstract contain at least one concrete number or result?*

---

## Standard skeleton

- **Title:** the specific finding or capability, not the general topic.
- **Abstract:** P7.
- **Introduction:** P4.
- **Results and discussion:** claims in logical order, each tied to a figure or table (P3).
- **Conclusions:** P6.
- **Methods / Supplemental Material:** everything needed to reproduce the work.

## Planning deliverable (task: Plan)

1. The main message in one sentence (P1)
2. The outline as claim sentences (P2)
3. A figure list, each with its one-sentence message (P3)
4. Gaps or missing evidence
